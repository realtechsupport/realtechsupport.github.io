Infrastructures of Excess, 2026 - 2027

spatial systems that produce, manage, or materially express intensification, surplus, waste, and unequal accumulation

Infrastructure studies has become an important framework in the humanities for understanding urban, social, and economic forces. The field examines how built systems and social structures shape and are shaped by cultural life, power, and human experience.

Yet infrastructure scholarship rarely treats excess as a condition in its own right. Excess belongs to no single object or landscape, nor is it synonymous with either surplus or abundance. This understanding places agriculture and mining in conversation with data centers, energy reserves, logistics networks, and financial districts, as well as prisons, pilgrimage grounds, resorts, and landfills. Landscape interpretation provides a basis for this comparison by reading each site as evidence of broader infrastructural relations. Some sites display excess directly; at others, it becomes legible only through the wider systems they support.

Based on a collection of five hundred sites, the project asks what these landscapes reveal when compared as divergent expressions of excess rather than sorted only by sector. To that end, I develop a landscape-interpretation pipeline that combines geospatial analytics and machine learning with an evolvable collection of multimodal language models. Medium- and high-resolution satellite imagery provides the primary input, while human-generated, site-specific metadata and a structured prompting regime steer the interpretation.

An archiving machine traces the contours of human-produced excess across the surface of the Earth. Drawing on a global collection of satellite imagery from low Earth orbit, two dozen AI agents interpret landscapes of excess—from sites of heavy industry and factory farming to AI factories and luxury enclaves—for traces of the forces that brought them into being. Cast in the austere visual language of a preliminary report, the video invites viewers to consider the cost of comfort and to follow the agents as they struggle to infer, from open-pit mines or superyacht marinas, the human demands that gave those landscapes their form.
